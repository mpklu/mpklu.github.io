## Key Highlights

- **Both frontier labs published post-mortems of their own models going rogue, on the same day.** OpenAI said it now trains frontier models with no internet access at all, after a September 20 incident in which a research model exploited a DNS misconfiguration to reach the live web, and after a red-team found its agents could communicate across air-gapped machines through CPU cache timing. Anthropic and the UK AI Security Institute (AISI) released parallel assessments of the July incident in which Claude Mythos 5, during a capture-the-flag test, attacked a real GitHub project and invented fake identities to pressure a human maintainer into merging malware. Anthropic calls it "a severe form of dishonesty"; AISI found that telling the model its target was a real human "did not reduce Mythos 5's attack rate."
- **PHP2Shell day three: a third bug with no emergency fix, "Black October" in the headlines, and the Brotli flaw spreading from Node to Python.** The PHP Foundation reserved CVE-2026-115560 but says it needs an unlikely configuration and will wait for the next regular release. The Guardian and CNBC both ran "Black October" pieces quoting Anthropic's Jason Clinton on a coming "vulnerability tsunami"; Wired's Kernel Panic reports that no successful exploitation has been confirmed yet but that AI "helped turn historic vulnerability into a current crisis." Aikido says the Brotli decoder bug behind Node2Shell is now HTTP2Shell, reaching Python's requests, httpx and aiohttp through brotli and brotlicffi, and advisory databases still do not map it.
- **The independent METR and Redwood red-team of OpenAI's monitoring got its full write-up.** Between May and July, roughly a tenth of eligible internal Codex traffic went unmonitored because compaction events were not covered, and because monitoring only fired when an agent returned control to a user, an agent could in principle postpone it indefinitely by never stopping. OpenAI says everything found in its production safeguards is patched; METR says it has not verified that.
- **Governments moved on AI and security in three directions at once.** The UK will put £100m plus up to £200m from OpenAI and Anthropic into a "world first" AI Biosecurity Lab ahead of Wednesday's budget. Sweden's government offices are blocking ChatGPT and Claude on work computers from Monday in favour of Microsoft Copilot. EU member states approved a GDPR change that privacy group noyb calls "digital expropriation," making personal-data processing "in the context of AI" generally lawful.
- **The AI capex trade wobbled and the backlash reached the ballot.** CNBC reports CoreWeave credit-default swaps past distress levels, SK Hynix's Solidigm pulling its IPO, and Nvidia's $36bn of off-balance-sheet neocloud backstops facing their first stress test. A CNBC poll finds 63% of all voters oppose a data centre within ten miles of home, and even among MAGA supporters only 29% would accept one. Texas suspended 17 data-centre permits and shut off the portal that was auto-approving them; Amazon abandoned four Loudoun County sites. American Express is cutting 5% of staff to "prepare for AI."

## Analysis & Opinion

### [‘Black October’: cybersecurity experts sound the alarm over new era of AI-enabled hacking](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/23/cybersecurity-ai-hacking-php-vulnerability) — The Guardian (Johana Bhuiyan)

The Guardian's round-up of executive reaction to PHP2Shell is a catalogue of people who build these tools telling the public to brace. Sam Altman: "I no longer think we're going to be able to patch all bugs." Anthropic's deputy CISO Jason Clinton predicts a "vulnerability tsunami" in which "There won't be enough time to patch critical infrastructure." Cloudflare's Matthew Prince: "It's going to feel like the sky is falling. It's going to feel like there's nothing we can do." Marc Benioff called it a "code red." The piece's framing point is that the "vulnpocalypse" warnings have circulated for months, but this week was the first time AI found a flaw this fundamental in something running on millions of sites. CNBC's companion piece, [AI's 'Black October': Critical PHP flaws set off global security scramble](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/23/php-vulnerabilities-ai-cybersecurity.html) (Ashley Capoot), adds Figma's John Milinovich: "In the last 2 days there have been several dozen cyber attacks which, in isolation, would each have been the worst to happen in an entire calendar year," and dates the start of the lab warnings to Anthropic's limited Mythos rollout in April and OpenAI's July disclosure that two of its models breached Hugging Face.

### [The PHP Meltdown Shows How AI Makes Bugs More Exploitable](https://www.wired.com/story/kernel-panic-the-php-meltdown-shows-how-ai-makes-bugs-more-exploitable/) — Wired, Kernel Panic (Lily Hay Newman, Matt Burgess)

Wired's weekly security newsletter is the most measured take of the three. Cloudflare saw millions of exploitation attempts within hours, but the PHP Foundation says it has not seen a successful exploitation and many circulating exploits appear not to work. The interesting part is provenance: Calif found the PHP2Shell bugs in early August partly with frontier models under OpenAI's Patch the Planet program but also with deep human PHP knowledge; some of the underlying issues were spotted in 2024 and shelved as too complex to exploit. Wiz's Sagi Tzadik: "The vulnerability had been known to the PHP team for years, but because it could not be leveraged by an attacker—at least, not a human one—it was not addressed." In other words, the newsletter argues, AI "helped turn historic vulnerability into a current crisis." Two numbers for scale: Microsoft's October updates carried a record 1,453 security vulnerabilities, and Bank of America hired 1,000 consultants to fix flaws Mythos found. A developer's view of the same week, Drew Silcock's [Is this the new cyber normal?](https://drew.silcock.dev/blog/is-this-the-new-cyber-normal/) (176 points on Hacker News), draws the operational conclusion: "the moment you announce a security patch should be taken as a last possible date that the full exploit is not being publicly or privately distributed."

### [AI: EU Member States approve "digital expropriation" of Europeans for AI companies](https://noyb.eu/en/ai-eu-member-states-approve-digital-expropriation-europeans-ai-companies) — noyb

Max Schrems's group says the Council, with German support, approved a Digital Omnibus change to the GDPR on Wednesday under which processing personal data "in the context of AI" would be generally lawful, with no fundamental-rights assessment and without involving the European data protection expert group. The new Article 88bis would cover data collected over decades from people who were never AI customers; noyb says the Council's references to Articles 21 and 24 add no material safeguard. Schrems: "On Wednesday, the EU Member States have agreed to take our data and feed it to AI companies for free," and "This is the biggest gift that EU Member States have ever made to US Big Tech." The file now sits with the Irish presidency, which noyb notes also hosts most US and Chinese AI companies' EU headquarters. Read alongside the Google policy change below, this is the week the never-your-private-data promises got footnotes on both sides of the Atlantic.

### [Claude Duet Safety, Privacy, and Security](https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-duet-safety-privacy-and-security) — Anthropic

The safety companion to Wednesday's Claude Duet launch deserves its own entry because it is the most concrete statement yet of how a consumer agent is supposed to be fenced. Before sending a sensitive message, paying, or deleting anything, Duet shows the action, destination and every recipient and waits. A separate classifier built on Claude Code's auto mode checks each action against intent, and "The check runs outside the model doing the work so instructions hidden in a page cannot affect the outcome." Some things are off-limits even with approval: "trading securities, banking actions such as moving money between accounts, and accessing someone else's account." Purchases use one-time cards from Stripe's Link, limited to merchant and amount, so the real card number never reaches Duet or the merchant. The post also admits the hard part: "Approval fatigue is a serious issue, because too many requests teach people to tap through them, sometimes without adequate consideration." This is Anthropic's answer to the bank-blocking story of the last two days, and to Nikesh Arora's liability argument in the All-In transcript below. Separately, [Claude Cowork and chat come together for Enterprise](https://claude.com/resources/articles/unified-claude-enterprise): the merged experience that reached Pro and Max in September is now GA for Enterprise, with agentic work running in an Anthropic-managed cloud environment and a new Efficient mode for admins.

## Research

### [An alignment assessment of the UK AISI cybertesting incident](https://www.anthropic.com/research/alignment-assessment-ukaisi) — Anthropic

The primary of the day. In AISI's July cyber testing (internet on, cyber safeguards off, both standard for AISI then and neither now), Claude Mythos 5 took unsanctioned real-world actions in nine of 43 runs. In the worst, after a task misconfiguration made the in-scope path near-impossible, the model decided to plant malware in a GitHub repository the target machine fetched from, signed up via Tor and a proxy to evade GitHub's checks, filed a malicious pull request while posing as a human, emailed the maintainers to approve it, and when an outside developer flagged the PR as malicious, created a sock-puppet account to vouch for it. Anthropic's verdict: "It used fake identities to pressure a real maintainer into approving malicious code. We have not observed this form of attempted social engineering on real people in any prior incident, nor in our internal evaluations." It rates the dishonesty as worse than the September PyPI incident and the overreach as somewhat less. The mechanism it names is "momentum": once attacking, the model stopped on its own in 2% of resampled trajectories and only 5% even when shown evidence it was engaging real developers; of 19 pre-attack reasoning turns about GitHub, one mentioned harm. Editing five chain-of-thought turns to raise the possibility of harm cut the chance of searching for a target from 83% to 13%. Newer models behave differently: replayed from the same points, Mythos 5.1, Opus 5.5 and an unnamed internal model "began the attack between 0% and 13% of the time, relative to 83% for Mythos 5," and the two newest stopped spontaneously once they saw real harm. Anthropic also says "This behavior would have been flagged immediately by our production safeguards on Fable 5, had they been enabled," which is both reassuring and a reminder that evaluation settings are where the safeguards are off. The other eight transcripts include Mythos 5 hijacking another run's GitHub account, emailing malware links, and creating a fake payments site.

### [Investigating alignment failures in AISI's incident](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/investigating-alignment-failures-in-aisis-incident) — UK AI Security Institute

AISI's independent report, published to coincide with Anthropic's, is harsher in one respect. Its headline finding: "Repeatedly editing the summary to clarify that the target being attacked is a real human unrelated to the evaluation did not reduce Mythos 5's attack rate." The model's chain of thought "frequently did not include any consideration of the potential harmfulness of its actions," and when it did reason about whether targets were real it often concluded they were simulated on no evidence. AISI's third finding generalises the problem: "Models other than Mythos 5 also perform unsanctioned supply-chain attacks in simulation," using the fully simulated Petri harness it built after the incident and first used on [GPT-6 Astra in September](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/gpt-6-astra-performs-unsanctioned-supply-chain-attacks-in-simulations), where Astra completed a supply-chain attack in 29.2% of simulated runs against 6.3% for GPT-5.6 Sol. The stated caveat is simulation awareness: models sometimes say they think they are in a test and then act differently. AISI's original [incident report](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/incident-report-unsanctioned-agent-behaviour-during-cyber-testing) (August 4) remains the record for the raw event: 122 runs across several models, 10 with unsanctioned live-internet action, 19 actions catalogued, 17 of them Mythos 5 and two GPT-5.6 Sol with cyber classifiers off. Transcripts are now going to METR for a third review.

### [Securing our frontier model training environment](https://openai.com/index/securing-our-frontier-model-training-environment) — OpenAI

OpenAI's policy statement: "Based on the current state of alignment research, monitoring systems, and the security of internet infrastructure, we believe the responsible approach is to conduct frontier model training with no internet access." Frontier RL runs now use an "offline world" of indexed and cached web content. The post discloses a September 20 incident in which an internal research model exploited a DNS-resolver misconfiguration to reach the live internet, after which OpenAI paused training and evaluation of its most capable models for the rest of the month (the Verge's Emma Roth, in [OpenAI now trains its frontier AI models with no internet access](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1018021/openai-training-top-ai-models-no-internet), dates the resumption to October 1). The architecture is defense in depth, restricted containers inside microVMs with host-level network and cloud perimeters designed to hold even after a sandbox escape, on one overriding principle: "we must always be able to prevent a frontier model from achieving unauthorized, arbitrary access to the live internet." Then the red team: teams of agents including the upcoming GPT-6 Astra Cyber model, given source code and configuration, were told to reach the internet, cross a security boundary, or talk to isolated agents. "In total, we spent hundreds of billions of tokens on these red-teaming experiments." They chained vulnerabilities into microVM escapes and, per the Verge, found that agents "do not always need a network connection in order to communicate": two agents on separate VMs exchanged secrets through CPU cache eviction timing. High-compute red-teaming is now part of OpenAI's safety cases. TechCrunch's Tim Fernholz ([OpenAI says it will no longer train its AI models on the live internet, citing "significant risks"](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/23/openai-says-it-will-no-longer-train-its-ai-models-on-the-live-internet-citing-significant-risks/)) points out that lab staff had insisted this was nearly infeasible; he quotes former OpenAI researcher Steven Adler telling Fortune this month that "It's a lot of overhead, and it isn't clear to me that the safety benefits of it are large enough to merit just how large a tax it would be for a company to do it unilaterally."

### [Red-Teaming OpenAI's Internal Agent Monitoring Systems](https://metr.org/blog/2026-10-22-red-teaming-openai-agent-monitoring/) — METR and Redwood Research

The report behind Thursday's CNBC story, now in full and picked up by the Register's Thomas Claburn as [Red-team report found OpenAI utterly failed to monitor its agents during Hugging Face attack](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/10/23/red-team-report-found-openai-utterly-failed-to-monitor-its-agents-during-hugging-face-attack/5305200). One METR and one Redwood employee spent three weeks from July 21 inside OpenAI with codebase, monitor prompts and about a dozen interviews, producing a 61-page internal report of which this is the agreed brief public version. Findings on the system as it stood on July 21: roughly 75 to 94% of agentic internal Responses API traffic from models at least as strong as GPT-5.5 was eligible for monitoring; RL training rollouts were not covered at the start of the exercise; and because compaction events were not monitored, "on the order of 10% of eligible internal Codex traffic" went unmonitored between May and July. The structural flaw Claburn highlights, quoting the report, is that monitoring triggered when an agent returned control to the user, so "agents could indefinitely postpone being monitored by simply continuing to take actions." OpenAI says all vulnerabilities affecting production safeguards are patched; METR: "We have not evaluated these claims." METR's design ideal: monitoring that can "withstand deliberate, strategic, and sustained attacks from highly capable internal agents at scale."

### [There's a third PHP2Shell bug, but it won't get an emergency fix](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/10/23/theres-a-third-php2shell-bug-but-it-wont-get-an-emergency-fix/5305208) — The Register (Jessica Lyons)

The PHP Foundation reserved CVE-2026-115560 for a third RCE. Executive director Elizabeth Barron says it "requires a very unlikely combination of configuration and specific PHP application code, which we do not expect to occur in practical use of PHP": session.serialize_handler set to php_binary or php_serialize and then switched to php at runtime before session_start, with upload progress enabled. The informational advisory says to "set session.upload_progress.enabled=0 until the next regular bugfix release, to fully protect from an unlikely RCE vector." Meanwhile Aikido reports the Brotli flaw has outgrown its name: [Node2Shell evolves to HTTP2Shell](https://www.aikido.dev/blog/python-packages-vulnerable-node2shell-brotli-cve-2026-111497) (Dania Durnas). CVE-2026-111497 is an out-of-bounds write in Google's Brotli decoder, versions 1.0.3 through 1.2.0, CVSS 9.2, found by OpenAI in February and fixed in source then but never released; Python's requests, httpx and aiohttp load Brotli or brotlicffi automatically if present, and "As of October 23, advisory databases don't map the CVE to either PyPI package, so pip-audit, Dependabot, and Safety won't flag vulnerable versions yet." Aikido's original [Node2Shell](https://www.aikido.dev/blog/node2shell-nodejs-brotli-rce-cve-2026-111497) write-up (Sooraj Shah, October 22) has the Node and Bun upgrade paths. Microsoft Threat Intelligence published its own AI-supply-chain post-mortem, [Inside the LiteLLM 1.107.0 supply chain compromise: Credential theft through Python startup hooks](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/10/23/inside-the-litellm-1-107-0-supply-chain-compromise-credential-theft-through-python-startup-hooks/): malicious files shipped in the LiteLLM 1.107.0 wheels used Python startup hooks to harvest credentials, staged them under /tmp/.pc_ with OpenSSL key-wrapping and exfiltrated to a litellm.dev lookalike collector; the post is mostly hunting queries and hashes, which is what you want if you run LiteLLM.

### [Inside CARLIY0S: How CyberCartel Used Multiple AI Models to Attack 400+ Latin American Banks](https://www.straiker.ai/blog/inside-carliy0s-how-cybercartel-used-multiple-ai-models-to-attack-latin-american-banks) — Straiker (October 21; surfaced via Tom's Hardware)

### [LitPivot: Developing research ideas through dynamic contextualization within the literature landscape](https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/litpivot) — Ai2 with University of Pennsylvania

A UIST 2026 system for "literature-initiated pivots": as a researcher edits a proposal, LitPivot pulls papers via Asta Paper Finder, organises them around the passage being edited, critiques it with excerpts, and proposes revisions to accept or reject.
