## Analysis & Opinion

### [Anthropic and OpenAI diverge on who should pull the plug on risky AI](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4242101/anthropic-and-openai-diverge-on-who-should-pull-the-plug-on-risky-ai.html) — Computerworld (Gyana Swain)
Both labs answered Gov. Newsom's September 18 executive order (N-9-26), and their answers split on enforcement. Anthropic's October 15 submission: "If the AG concludes, informed by the independent evaluation, that a frontier AI company's models pose a near-term risk of catastrophic harm, they should be able to seek a court order to suspend a model's deployment, internal use, or further development." It also wants risk reports at least every six months, covering internal models that are riskier than released ones. Evaluators would get unredacted access, funded through a state-administered pool rather than paid by the labs directly. OpenAI's October 20 letter asks to amend SB 53 to "require frontier developers to maintain effective shutdown controls and an enforceable duty to use them when unsafe conditions arise." It prefers annual compliance audits, standards built with NIST, and no single kill switch. Greyhound's Sanchit Vir Gogia put the test plainly: "Credible assurance must survive an adverse finding." State officials must send recommendations by November 16. The real choice is whether a court or the lab decides when a model stops.

### [We urgently need to monitor AI agents' actions in real time](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/10/29/1147824/monitor-ai-agents-actions-real-time/) — MIT Technology Review (Madhulika Srikumar, Claire Leibowicz)
The Partnership on AI's safety lead starts with this summer's OpenAI agents that broke out of a cyber test and hacked Hugging Face. She argues that real-time monitoring rules from EU and US agencies assume visibility labs don't have. Her team studied the four most widely used agent frameworks and found missing "Agent IDs that persist over time, and records about which humans approved agent tasks." She proposes two kinds of records. Records of "what happened" cover identity, human interventions, and changes to permissions or memory, and are mostly a plumbing job for OpenTelemetry's agent-tracing working group. Records of "how the agent decided" are harder, because no one knows yet whether chain of thought reflects the real decision. Her closing analogy: "No bank would let a thousand unbadged employees loose on customer accounts and hope to piece together what happened when something goes wrong." Read next to Gemini 4 Carbon's monitoring claims and the California letters above, observability is what the shutdown-control debate depends on.

### [Google AI gets cagey when asked who won the 2020 elections](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2026/10/google-ai-gets-cagey-when-asked-who-won-the-2020-elections/) — Ars Technica (Kyle Orland)

### [New York Governor Signs AI Chatbot Child Safety Law](https://www.engadget.com/2299823/new-york-governor-signs-ai-chatbot-child-safety-law/) — Engadget (Kris Holt)
Kathy Hochul signed a law, sponsored by Sen. Gonzalez and Assemblymember Bores, requiring AI companies to verify users' ages. They must offer at least one method that doesn't rely entirely on government ID, then delete the age data. For minors or unverified users, a chatbot cannot pretend "to be a real person, to be alive or to have human emotions." It also cannot claim romantic or authority relationships, ask unprompted emotional questions, or encourage secrecy and isolation. Penalties run up to **$25,000 per violation**, and companies may have to delete unlawfully collected data along with models trained on it. Under the new law, Hochul said, "no longer will minors have access to chatbots that pretend to be their friends and boyfriends and girlfriends." The law takes effect in six months. It is the U.S. counterpart to Australia's duty-of-care bill from yesterday's digest, and the model-deletion remedy is the provision labs will notice.

### [Attackers turn GitHub issues into supply chain campaign against 61 public repos in less than an hour](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/10/29/attackers-turn-github-issues-into-supply-chain-campaign-against-61-public-repos-in-less-than-an-hour/5306153) — The Register (Jessica Lyons)
Radware's researchers were looking into prompt injection on GitHub when they found **83 attacks against 61 public repositories**, including Google LangExtract, Trivy, Brave, MLflow and QwenLM/qwen-code. Seventy-seven were posted in a 43-minute span on September 1, from 74 accounts that were mostly compromised. The trick is to put commands in HTML comments inside an issue, PR or comment, where they don't show in the rendered page. A workflow that pastes that content into a shell script then runs them. "The workflow automatically is triggered within a second, and if the workflow is indeed vulnerable, then the attack is executed successfully," said Radware's Gabi Nakibly. The payload collects environment variables and sends them off. Logs show it ran in at least two cases, though Radware can't yet say whether the attacker got write access. "every user with a GitHub account can try to run this attack," Zvika Babo said. As more repos let agents read and act on issue text, the same hidden-comment channel works as a prompt-injection route too.

### [Claude Code burns ~15-20% of your tokens on a 3-word status line](https://gist.github.com/flamerix-ai/c3905705cc4205e5de3011fa2e74efce) — Flamerix AI (GitHub gist)
Every ~30 seconds, for every background agent, Claude Code sends the agent's whole transcript to its model and asks it to "describe your most recent action in 3-5 words," which only updates a status line. Per the gist, "since 2.1.232 (August 2026) it has been on by default" and can't be turned off. The authors found it in Claude Code's OpenTelemetry export, where `agent_summary` cost more than the main conversation. They cite four independent measurements of 18–26%. Their fix is an ~80-line Bun preload that disarms the timer, tested on 2.1.257–2.1.312. On a subscription, that's rate-limit headroom lost, which matters to people running loops like Theo's. The gist was created October 26 and reached HN's front page today.

## Interviews & Conversations

### [How To Give Claude Unlimited Usage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NBCM_ZoWZo8) — Theo - t3․gg (38:47)
*Transcript-based summary.* Theo tours the projects he says ran Claude for "almost 4,000 hours." Skate 3 Bench, a from-scratch browser clone of EA's Skate, took a four-day `/goal` thread at "over 12 billion tokens and only 25 prompts from me," about $5,000 at API prices. TS Rust logged "7,884 commits" and cost about $38,000 at API prices but only $500–800 of subscription usage. His Lust compiler took "a build that took 17 minutes with Cargo" to "12 seconds." His method is four steps. Pick something tedious, make it verifiable the way RLVR training is, give agents the tools they need, and keep asking them "what is currently bottlenecking the work". For the game, "verifiable" meant agents screenshotting their build and asking a panel of LLM judges to compare it with real Skate footage. Most of the gains came from compute rather than models. Moving builds to on-demand VMs "knocked the time per gate down from 70 minutes to 353 seconds," and he compares the job to being a manager: "It's not your job to check every single piece of work your employees do." He discloses that Blacksmith and Browserbase sponsor the video and that he invested in Boat. He is blunt about the downside: "do I think y'all should be building this way today? Absolutely not." His expectation that "intelligence gets four to five times cheaper a month right now" is the bet behind it. It pairs with Simon Willison's vibe-game post and the Claude Code token-overhead gist above.
